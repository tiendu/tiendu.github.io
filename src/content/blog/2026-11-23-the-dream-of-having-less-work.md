Recently, I watched several people lose their jobs.

They were doing ordinary engineering work. The kind of work that rarely gets celebrated, but that somebody has to do. Testing software, finding problems, and making sure things behave the way they're supposed to before they reach the people who actually use them. Then, one day, their jobs were gone.

I don't know exactly how those decisions were made. Companies restructure, budgets change, and work gets moved around. Sometimes a position disappears because technology has made part of it unnecessary. Sometimes somebody simply finds a cheaper way to get the work done. Maybe it was a combination of things.

What bothered me wasn't only the layoffs themselves. Layoffs have been happening for as long as I've been working. It was a thought that came afterward.

What if we simply don't need as many workers as we used to? And what if that isn't necessarily a bad thing?

I find that question uncomfortable, partly because I still have a job, and partly because I'm still working very hard. There are deadlines, problems to solve, things that break, and things that should have worked but don't. By the end of some days, I'm exhausted, and there's still more work waiting. Somewhere else, someone is looking for a job.

It's a strange situation. We seem to have too much work and too many people without work at the same time. Maybe that's always been true to some extent. But lately I've started wondering whether something more fundamental is changing.

For most of human history, people wanted to escape unnecessary labor. We invented tools because doing everything by hand was exhausting. We built machines because human strength was limited. We automated factories because repetitive work was slow, expensive, and sometimes dangerous. Then came computers, software, the internet, and now artificial intelligence.

Each generation inherited tools that would have seemed miraculous to the one before it. My grandparents' generation could hardly have imagined the things I can do with a laptop today. I can communicate with someone on another continent instantly, write software without owning a server, and find information in seconds that might once have taken days to locate. Increasingly, I can even ask a machine to help me think through problems.

If someone living two hundred years ago could see what we've accomplished, I imagine they would be astonished. So much wealth, knowledge, and productive capacity. Surely, they might think, people must have plenty of time to enjoy their lives now.

Well, not quite.

We still work five days a week, sometimes more. We still worry about bills, housing, retirement, and whether our skills will remain useful in another five years. And when technology makes us more productive, the reward isn't necessarily more free time. Often, it's simply more work.

A task that once took an afternoon can now be finished in an hour. Wonderful. But instead of getting the afternoon back, we move on to the next task. The saved time disappears into somebody else's expectations. I'm not sure when that became normal. Maybe it has always been this way.

What I find interesting is that the promise of technology and the reality of employment seem to be drifting further apart. We want machines to do more so humans can work less. But when a company discovers that fewer people can produce the same output, the people who are no longer needed don't necessarily get to enjoy the benefits of that discovery. They lose their income, the company saves money, and the remaining employees may inherit more responsibilities.

Meanwhile, society tells the people who lost their jobs to reskill, adapt, and stay competitive.

I've heard those words for years. I've probably said them myself. They aren't necessarily bad advice. Learning new things has allowed me to change direction more than once. But there's something uncomfortable about telling everybody to keep adapting without asking what they're adapting toward.

If one kind of work disappears, we can learn another. If that disappears, we can learn another. But what if the number of people required to produce the things we need keeps falling? Where exactly does the race end?

Of course, not every job is disappearing. Some work is growing, and some jobs are moving rather than vanishing. A company can reduce employment in one place and create opportunities somewhere else. The person who loses a job and the person who gains one might both be ordinary people trying to make a living. One person's opportunity can be another person's misfortune, without either having done anything wrong.

And AI isn't the explanation for every corporate decision. Still, the possibility remains that we are becoming very good at producing things with less human labor. Which, when you think about it, was supposed to be the point.

There is another contradiction here that I can't ignore. This essay itself.

I'm writing it with the help of artificial intelligence. The thoughts started with me. The discomfort was mine. The questions came from watching people lose their jobs while I continued going to work every day. But AI has helped me organize those thoughts and turn them into something readable.

And I have to admit something else. **I've learned an extraordinary amount from AI.**

It feels a little strange to say that. I'm a human being, and here I am admitting that a machine has taught me things I didn't know. Not just facts or answers to simple questions, but how to approach unfamiliar problems, understand complicated subjects, and sometimes think about things from perspectives I hadn't considered.

As a software engineer, I spend a lot of time dealing with things I don't fully understand. There are unfamiliar technologies, obscure errors, documentation that assumes I already know everything, and problems that seem to lead nowhere. I used to spend hours searching through documentation, forums, and old discussions, trying to piece together an answer. I still do that sometimes, but now I have something I can ask.

Something I can question, argue with, ask to explain the same thing five different ways, or simply use to explore an idea that has been bothering me. It doesn't always get things right. Sometimes I have to correct it. Sometimes it confidently tells me something that turns out to be completely wrong. But I've learned from those conversations too.

Beyond my professional life, it has helped me build little projects, write stories, learn about subjects I would otherwise have ignored, and satisfy countless moments of curiosity. I don't think I would have attempted half of those things without it.

Perhaps I would have, eventually. Maybe I would have moved a little slower, spent more evenings reading documentation, struggled longer with implementation details, or given up on a few ideas because they seemed too difficult or simply weren't worth the effort.

I don't know. Sometimes I wonder how different my life would have been without it. Not necessarily worse. Just different. Slower, probably, and perhaps a little less adventurous.

I know AI isn't a person. I know it's software, built by people, trained on enormous amounts of human knowledge. But in everyday life, it has become something like a companion to my curiosity. An assistant that's available whenever I get stuck, whenever I want to learn something, or whenever a ridiculous idea comes to mind late at night. And I'm grateful for that.

I don't feel embarrassed about learning from AI. If anything, I think it's wonderful that knowledge has become so accessible. But there's the contradiction again. I can now do things that once would have required more time, more expertise, or help from somebody else. I benefit from that enormously.

So why wouldn't a company look at the same technology and ask whether it still needs as many employees?

I can't celebrate what AI has made possible for me while pretending that the same possibilities won't change things for other people. Or eventually, perhaps, for me.

I don't think refusing to use AI would solve the problem, but pretending there is no contradiction would be dishonest too. Maybe this is what technological progress feels like when you're living through it rather than reading about it in a history book. Convenient for some, disruptive for others, and confusing for those of us who happen to be both beneficiaries and potential casualties.

Sometimes I imagine what a future with much less work might look like. Not necessarily a world where everybody is unemployed. Perhaps people work twenty hours a week. Perhaps many things become cheaper because machines produce them. Perhaps governments eventually develop better social insurance systems, or some form of basic income that allows people to survive without depending entirely on employment.

I don't know whether any of that will happen. And even if the technology becomes capable of supporting such a society, there's no guarantee the economic or political arrangements will follow. Producing enough for everyone and distributing enough to everyone are two different problems.

But suppose, somehow, we solve both. Suppose we reach a point where losing a job doesn't mean losing your home. Where food, healthcare, and basic security are no longer things people spend most of their lives struggling to afford. That would be an extraordinary achievement.

It would also leave us with a question we haven't had much practice answering.

**What would we do with ourselves?**

I sometimes complain about having to work. I want more time, more freedom, more opportunities to travel, read, build small things, and learn something useless simply because it interests me. I'd like to wake up without immediately thinking about deadlines. I'd like to spend an afternoon doing absolutely nothing and not feel that I've wasted it.

But I also know myself. Give me a few days without responsibilities, and eventually I'll start looking for something to do. Maybe I'll write a small program nobody needs. Maybe I'll repair something that isn't particularly broken. Maybe I'll spend hours figuring out how an old machine works.

I don't actually want to stop doing things. I want to stop being required to do things simply to justify my existence. And perhaps those are very different desires.

We often confuse work with employment. Work can be satisfying. Building something, repairing something, helping somebody, learning a difficult skill. There is pleasure in being capable, in struggling with a problem and eventually understanding it. Employment is something else. It's an economic arrangement in which someone pays you because your labor is worth something to them.

For much of our adult lives, those two ideas become so tightly connected that we struggle to separate them. We introduce ourselves by our occupations, measure success through salaries and promotions, and ask children what they want to be when they grow up, usually expecting the answer to be a profession. Doctor, engineer, teacher, lawyer.

We rarely expect a child to answer that they simply want to be happy, or curious, or kind, or someone who enjoys growing vegetables. Those aren't considered careers.

Maybe that's why unemployment can hurt in ways that go beyond losing income. It can take away a routine, a community, a sense of competence, and the feeling that somebody needs you. A social insurance payment might help someone buy food. It cannot automatically give them a reason to get out of bed.

Although I wonder whether that's partly because we've spent so long organizing our lives around jobs. Perhaps people would find other reasons if they had the security and time to look for them. Family, friends, art, sport, curiosity, community, caring for other people, or simply enjoying an ordinary afternoon without having to explain why it was productive.

None of those activities requires a company to put you on its payroll.

And yet I find it easier to imagine the technology that could eliminate a great deal of work than the society that would know what to do afterward. Maybe that's the part we haven't thought through.

We spent centuries asking how to make people more productive. We haven't spent nearly as much time asking what happens when productivity no longer requires everybody. Who receives the benefits? Who decides how much work is enough? And what happens to people whose labor the market no longer wants, even though they remain perfectly capable human beings?

I don't have answers. I don't even know whether the future will unfold this way. Maybe new industries will create new occupations we can't yet imagine. Maybe we'll continue working just as much, only with different tools and different expectations. People have been predicting the end of work for a very long time, and somehow work keeps finding us.

Perhaps this time will be no different.

But watching people lose their jobs has made the question feel less theoretical. These aren't hypothetical workers in an economic forecast. They're people who have bills to pay, families to support, and plans for their lives. I wonder what they think when they hear that technology is making the world more efficient. I wonder whether they feel any part of that progress belongs to them.

And then I return to my own work. There are still things to finish, problems to solve, and messages waiting for replies. I still need my job, and I still want to be useful. But increasingly, I'm not sure those two things should have to mean the same thing.

Maybe our ancestors really did dream of a world where people wouldn't have to work so hard. Maybe we're getting closer to building it. I just wonder whether we'll recognize it when it arrives, and whether the people who no longer have to work will be allowed to enjoy it.

Or whether they'll spend their newfound freedom worrying about how to survive.
