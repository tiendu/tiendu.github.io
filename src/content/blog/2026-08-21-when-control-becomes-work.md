I once worked with a micromanager.

Not the dramatic kind who screams at people or openly says, "I don't trust you."

That would almost be easier.

This was the quieter version.

What is the status?

Have you checked this?

Can you update me when you're done?

Did you see my message?

Before you do that, can you run it by me?

After you do that, can you let me know?

Sometimes I would receive a question about something I was already working on, answer it, continue working, and then receive another question asking where I was with the thing I had just answered about.

At first I did what I imagine most people do.

I tried to become more independent.

I gave concise updates. I tried to anticipate questions. I solved small problems without bothering anyone. If there were three possible ways to do something and one was obviously reasonable, I picked it and moved on.

Strangely, that made things worse.

Because the issue was never really lack of information.

The issue was control.

And control is difficult to satisfy because there is always one more thing that could theoretically be checked.

Eventually I stopped fighting it.

If you want updates, I can give updates.

Lots of updates.

If a decision apparently requires approval, then I will not quietly make the decision anymore.

"Do you prefer A or B?"

"If I proceed with this, are you okay with it?"

"I've completed the first part. Would you like me to continue with the second?"

"Just confirming that this is what you had in mind."

Nothing rude.

Nothing sarcastic.

Nothing that could reasonably be called insubordination.

Quite the opposite.

I became extremely cooperative.

And something funny happened.

Micromanagement started becoming work for the micromanager.

Every decision they wanted to own now actually belonged to them.

Every checkpoint created another thing they had to read.

Every request for visibility produced more visibility.

Every unnecessary approval created an approval waiting in their inbox.

The pattern became obvious in very ordinary situations.

Suppose someone keeps asking for status updates every couple of hours.

Normally, I might just continue working and send a meaningful update when something actually changes.

But if they want constant visibility, fine.

"Started investigating."

"Found two possible causes."

"Testing the first one."

"First one ruled out."

"Moving to the second."

"Still running."

"No change yet."

Suddenly the person asking for visibility discovers that visibility itself produces information they now have to process.

Or suppose someone insists that I should not make changes without asking first.

Fine.

Then even small decisions stop being mine.

"I can solve this by changing the configuration or by changing the application logic. Which direction would you prefer?"

"Do you want the quick workaround or the proper fix?"

"This warning is harmless. Do you want me to leave it or spend time removing it?"

They wanted authority over the decision.

Now they get the decision.

All of it.

Another common one is vague instructions followed by very specific criticism.

"Can you make this better?"

Better how?

Shorter?

More detailed?

More technical?

More polished?

Different structure?

In the past, I might have interpreted what they meant, done the work, and hoped I guessed correctly.

With a micromanager, that is a trap.

So instead:

"When you say better, which of these are you looking for?"

Now the ambiguity belongs to the person who created it.

Or there is the classic request to "keep me in the loop."

That phrase can mean almost anything.

So I keep them in the loop.

Aggressively.

"I've contacted them."

"They replied."

"I replied to their reply."

"They've acknowledged my reply."

"Nothing else is needed right now."

There is a certain point at which people suddenly discover that perhaps they did not actually want to be in every loop.

Another favorite is when someone asks you to finish something early and then immediately questions whether you finished it too early.

"Is this final?"

"Yes."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

"Did you check everything?"

"Yes."

"Maybe we should review it together."

Of course.

Let's review the completed work that was delivered ahead of schedule because apparently finishing early has created a new problem.

Or someone schedules a meeting to prepare for another meeting.

Then perhaps another small sync is needed to make sure everyone is aligned before the preparation meeting for the actual meeting.

At some point, I stopped protecting people from the absurdity of that process.

If the meeting exists, I attend.

If another alignment is needed, sure.

If we need a pre-alignment before the alignment, send me the invite.

Eventually the calendar itself starts explaining the problem better than I ever could.

There is also the manager who wants to be copied on everything.

Okay.

They get copied.

Not selectively.

Not only when something is important.

Everything.

Every thread.

Every update.

Every confirmation.

Every reply.

If someone insists that they need full context, I am not going to spend extra effort deciding which context they actually meant.

Another one is when the manager asks to review work before it is ready.

This one used to annoy me because unfinished work is supposed to be unfinished.

But fine.

"This is the current draft. It is incomplete. The wording is temporary. Two sections are placeholders. The numbers are not final."

Then you send it.

And inevitably:

"This section feels unfinished."

Yes.

It is unfinished.

That is why I said it was unfinished.

Or they tell you to ask for approval before taking action, but later complain that you ask too many questions.

That one is almost beautiful.

"You asked me to confirm these changes before proceeding, so I'm confirming."

There is really nowhere for the conversation to go after that.

Another common case is priority management.

Everything is urgent.

Everything is high priority.

Everything needs to be done today.

I used to try to silently rearrange things and somehow make it work.

Not anymore.

"I currently have A, B, and C marked as urgent. Which one should move to the top?"

That question is surprisingly powerful.

Because now the person assigning the work has to confront something employees deal with constantly:

priorities require trade-offs.

You cannot have five number-one priorities.

If everything is urgent, then nothing is prioritized.

There is also the manager who keeps changing direction midway through a task.

Do this.

Actually, do that.

Wait, go back to the first thing.

Why isn't it finished yet?

That is another place where I stopped absorbing the cost.

"Just confirming that we're switching from A to B. I'll pause A and continue with B."

Then later:

"Just confirming that we're switching back from B to A. B will remain incomplete."

Very simple.

Very factual.

Very difficult to rewrite history afterward.

And that is probably the most useful part of all this.

Micromanagement often thrives on invisible work.

The employee remembers the changed requirements.

The employee reconciles conflicting instructions.

The employee silently reprioritizes.

The employee guesses what "better" means.

The employee decides which updates are worth sending.

The employee protects the manager from the consequences of their own management style.

I eventually stopped doing some of that invisible work.

Not because I wanted to sabotage anything.

Quite the opposite.

I wanted the actual process to become visible.

If you change the requirement three times, there should be three changes visible somewhere.

If you want approval authority, there should be approvals waiting for you.

If you create five urgent tasks, you should have to decide which one is actually first.

If you want constant updates, there should be constant updates.

If you want to attend every decision, then there should be decisions waiting for you.

That was when I understood something about micromanagement that I had missed before.

It feels cheap when somebody else carries the cost.

A manager can ask for another status update in ten seconds.

The employee has to stop what they are doing, reconstruct the context, explain it, wait for a response, possibly adjust direction, then rebuild the mental state they had before the interruption.

From the manager's side, it feels like a small question.

From the other side, enough small questions can become the workday.

So I simply stopped absorbing that cost invisibly.

If you want to participate in every small decision, you are welcome to participate in every small decision.

I will make room for you.

Eventually the questions became less frequent.

Some decisions suddenly did not require approval anymore.

I started hearing phrases like:

"That's fine, just use your judgment."

Interesting.

My judgment had apparently returned.

I don't think this is some brilliant technique for defeating bad managers. Deliberately annoying people at work is a stupid game, and malicious compliance can easily become more exhausting than the original problem.

But the experience changed how I think about control.

Sometimes people continue a behavior because someone else quietly makes that behavior convenient for them.

The employee fills in the gaps.

The employee interprets the vague instruction.

The employee makes the real decision.

The employee does the thinking.

Then the manager appears at the end to approve it.

That arrangement allows someone to feel involved in everything without actually carrying the weight of being involved in everything.

Once that weight becomes visible, control becomes much less attractive.

These days, when someone wants ownership of a decision, I am quite happy to give it to them.

Completely.

Maybe that sounds passive-aggressive.

Maybe sometimes it is.

But there is also something strangely fair about it.

You wanted the steering wheel.

Here you go.
